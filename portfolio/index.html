Case 04

A checkout that looked broken

JD Sports Group (Sprinter and Sportzone) · Senior Digital & Marketing Data Analyst · 2021–2024

The business wanted the checkout redesigned. Before anyone committed engineering time, I compared the funnel step by step with the retail benchmark, and found a page carrying of checkout traffic that had never been tracked. I fixed that first.

Three teams wanted three different fixes. Marketing wanted Customer Care on video calls, Customer Care wanted chat on product pages, and the evidence pointed at the checkout. I showed each team, step by step, what data existed at each stage and what each option would tell us, and we started with the checkout.

Then we went after the reasons, with a Hotjar survey categorised through the OpenAI API and live chat reasons logged by Customer Care in Salesforce. Payment failures came out as the main cause. After the changes shipped, checkout conversion rose against the pre-launch baseline, and Customer Care kept using the weekly report in its own meetings.

Case 08

What goes first in the product grid

JD Sports Group (Sprinter) · Senior Digital & Marketing Data Analyst · 2021–2024

The order of a category page decides which products customers see first. The performance team asked to test new ranking algorithms in Sprinter's internal merchandising tool: one that reordered the grid from what the visitor had been browsing, and one that pushed up products related to those they had looked at.

It was a true A/B test: visitors were split at random into groups that saw each ordering at the same time, so the comparison wasn't skewed by season or campaigns, unlike a before-and-after. Success was revenue per visitor, with clicks on the grid and add-to-cart as early signals.

The hard part was the unit of the split. Splitting by user is the right choice here: the personalised ordering learns from what a visitor browses, so a split by session can show the same person both versions on different visits and blur the difference. But recognising a returning visitor needs a cookie, and on Sprinter that cookie depended on consent, so the technical team couldn't split by user and the test ran by session, against a control group with the current ordering.

How I'd check a test like this today: three arms (the current ordering as control and the two algorithms) need a multivariate test with a correction for multiple comparisons. Clicks and add-to-cart are proportions, so a two-proportion test fits. Revenue per visitor is skewed by a few big baskets, so it needs a test that copes with that, such as Welch's t-test on a large sample or a bootstrap. And with a split by session, the session has to be the unit of analysis too. My free calculator covers the planning, the multivariate set-up, several metrics at once and the risk of picking the wrong winner.

Case 09

Can the clinic's numbers be trusted?

Personal project · dbt and DuckDB on synthetic data · 2026

A practice project on how I'd structure analytics a team can trust. Fourteen business questions about a fictional clinic, from 30-day readmissions to medication adherence, each answered by its own dbt model on synthetic data. No real patients are involved.

The data comes with defects planted on purpose: duplicated intake forms, a physician with no department, stays discharged before they were admitted and impossible lab values. Bad rows are flagged, not hidden, and each model decides what to exclude. and a data quality scorecard catch the defects: known ones warn without blocking the build, real breaks fail it.

It also works as a data catalogue. Every model has an owner, a data classification and its personal data columns tagged, and the lineage graph runs from the raw data to the reports that use it. A check in the pipeline fails the build if is left undocumented.

How it was built, with AI agents, is in Agentic AI in my own work.
